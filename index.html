The connected classroom

Opening and national context

Digital technology is now part of almost every layer of schooling, from learning platforms and online assessment to generative artificial intelligence. Yet being surrounded by technology is not the same as knowing how to use it well. Australia's latest national test of ICT literacy puts that gap into context.

In 2025, half of Year 6 students (50%) reached the proficient standard, down from 55% in 2022. Among Year 10 students the share was 37%, down from 46% in 2022 and the lowest since testing began. ACARA noted that these results sit alongside students reporting extensive everyday experience with digital tools.

That contrast shapes the question for this report. If use is high but proficiency is falling, then access to devices cannot be the whole story. A student may own a device but lack a reliable connection, suitable learning resources, or the confidence and guidance to use tools critically. Location, household circumstances and the school a child attends may all shape what is possible.

This investigation discusses the scale of the system, access, geography, student groups, change over time, and what an equitable digital classroom could require.

The scale of Australian schooling

Before looking for differences it helps to see the system's shape. ACARA's 2025 school profile covers 9,755 schools, and together they enrol about 4.2 million students.

Independent schools are the fewest at 1,248, but their average campus (617 students) is the largest of the three sectors, against 459 for Catholic and 393 for government schools. This matters for what follows. Policy aimed at government schools reaches most students, but sectors differ in size and, as later charts show, in the advantage of their communities.

ICT literacy is moving in the wrong direction nationwide

Reading the chart

Each line follows one year level across three assessment rounds (2017, 2022 and 2025). The shaded band highlights the latest change, and the labels give the point change since 2022: five points for Year 6 and nine for Year 10.

The two year levels began almost level in 2017 (53% and 54%) and have since diverged, as the Year 10 line falls steeply while Year 6 has only dipped. The result establishes that measured proficiency has dropped but it doesn't tell us why, and it does not show that students have less access to devices. The remaining sections look for context that could help explain who is most exposed to this decline.

Access is the starting point

Devices, connectivity and affordability

Digital inclusion is a precondition for digital learning. The Australian Digital Inclusion Index (ADII) scores Access, Affordability and Digital Ability on a 0 to 100 scale, using a 2024 survey of Australian households. It describes the whole population, framing the environment students live in rather than measuring students directly.

Nationally the ADII is improving. The 2025 total index score is 73.6, and Access has risen 4.8 points since 2023 to 76.8 but, about one in five Australians (20.6%) are still excluded or highly excluded, and 9.7% rely on a mobile connection alone. For schoolwork, that matters: the ADII notes that smartphones can support basic participation but are not designed for many study tasks, which need a laptop or desktop computer. Around 4% of Australians say their household lacks enough devices for everyone's needs, and the rate is higher for people who are unemployed (15.6%), did not finish secondary school (10.8%) or live in public housing (10.8%).

Reading the chart

Darker blue as per the legend in the bottom left that represents the ADII total scores creates a higher total index score and each statistic is split by state.

The spread across jurisdictions is about ten points, from 69.4 in Tasmania to 79.5 in the ACT. Five of the eight sit within two points of the national score (73.6), hence the story is less a uniform gradient than a small high-scoring territory, a cluster near the average and a tail of lower-scoring jurisdictions led by Tasmania and South Australia (71.3). The ACT is small and almost entirely urban, which makes its score not a model for the rest to be effectivley compared against. The ADII itself ties state differences partly to socioeconomic composition, for example Tasmania's lower median personal income and older population, which the map shows where inclusion is lower without isolating the reasons.

Access is not the same as ability

A connection is only useful if people can afford and use it. ADII Access and Digital Ability do not always move together, and the gap between them changes with remoteness.

Reading the chart

Each row compares the two scores for one remoteness area. In major cities, Access (78.3) sits about three points above Digital Ability (75.4). In very remote areas the order reverses: Access is 57.4 and Digital Ability 63.6, so Access trails by 6.2 points, the orange annotation. Remote areas are close to level, and inner and outer regional areas have Access about four to five points above ability.

In very remote regions and locations the infrastructure is the binding constraint; the ADII reports an Access score 19.4 points below the national average there. Skills cannot make up for a connection that is missing or unreliable. The ADII also finds that network interruptions are far more common outside cities, reaching 30.5% in very remote areas.

Where opportunity differs

Geographic and socioeconomic patterns

Place shapes both where schools are and who they serve. This section compares states, then maps the school system itself. The school-level charts use remoteness and the Index of Community Socio-Educational Advantage (ICSEA) as indirect context, not as measures of technology access.

Where the schools are

Reading the chart

One dot is one school. Dense clusters follow the population: around Sydney and the east coast, Melbourne and the south-east, Adelaide, and Perth. The interior is left almost blank, with small settlements marked by isolated dots. The map says where school infrastructure exists, which is the geography any national digital strategy must serve. Dot density says nothing about the quality of a school's connection.

How states compare on each dimension

Reading the chart

Rows are jurisdictions and the two area types, columns are the three dimensions, and colour shows the signed gap to the national score (blue above, orange below). Tasmania is below the national figure on all three dimensions, with the widest shortfall on Affordability (4.9 points). The Northern Territory has the largest state-level shortfall, 5.1 points on Access, yet scores above the national average on Affordability and Digital Ability, which is why the ADII calls its position a mixed picture.

The largest gap on the chart is the 5.3-point shortfall in Digital Ability for the rest of Australia outside capital cities, set against capital cities 2.6 points above. Region matters most for skills and support, not only for infrastructure. As the ADII cautions, remote mining centres are a notable exception, with relatively higher scores because of concentrations of higher-income earners.

Students are even more concentrated than schools

Nearly three in four students (73.6%) attend school in a major city, and government schools in major cities alone account for 45.5% of all students. Remote and very remote areas together hold 1.8% of students, too few to label as tiles. One system, different needs

Resources, advantage and who is using new tools

Schools do not start from the same place. This section looks at three things that bear on whether digital learning is accessible and not merely available: the staffing capacity to support students, the spread of community advantage between schools, and which groups are adopting the newest tools.

Capacity to support students

Reading the chart

Each line is a state or territory, ranked each year from fewest students per teacher (rank 1) to most. The Northern Territory is first throughout, with 11.0 students per FTE teacher in 2025. Tasmania, highlighted in orange, climbs from seventh in 2015 to second in 2025, as its ratio falls from 14.1 to 11.8. Western Australia sits at the bottom in 2025 with 13.7, though it too improved by 0.6 over the decade.

This is a staffing indicator, not a technology one, and a rank shows relative position rather than the size of a change. It is included because skilled teachers are what turn devices into learning, an area where the ICT results suggest the system is not yet succeeding. Low ratios in small, remote jurisdictions often reflect small schools rather than extra resources.

Averages hide wide differences between schools

Reading the chart

Each dot is a school, grouped by remoteness and coloured by sector, and the dark bar is the median for all schools in that group. Median ICSEA steps down from 1,049 in major cities to 982 in inner regional, about 951 in outer regional and 930 in remote areas, then drops sharply to 707 in very remote areas. The spread within each group is large: major cities contain schools as low as roughly 670 and regional groups include schools above 1,000.

Two cautions apply. First, the overlap means remoteness alone does not decide a school's advantage. Second, ICSEA describes school communities, not devices or internet. It becomes relevant here because the national ICT results show continuing gaps between groups of students, and parental education and occupation are among the factors linked with stronger results. Very remote schools have the lowest advantage by a wide margin.

Who is using generative AI?

Reading the chart

Each dot shows the share of a group who recently used a generative AI tool, and the stem runs to the national average of 45.6%. Students are the heaviest users (78.9%), followed by 18 to 34 year olds (69.1%), people with a bachelor's degree (62.0%) and people who speak another language at home (58.8%). Below the line sit people with disability (33.9%), First Nations people (36.6%) and people who left school at Year 10 (4.2%).

For schools, the useful message is that adoption is outpacing guidance. ACARA's results show students using digital tools heavily while proficiency falls, and the ADII warns that uneven skills could turn new tools into another channel of inequality. A group's low use of AI here is not evidence about school-based access, but it flags who may be left behind as these tools become part of study.

The changing classroom

Change over time, and the limits of what we can conclude

The classroom of 2025 looks very different from that of a decade ago. Digital access has improved: the ADII Access score rose 4.8 points between 2023 and 2025, and Digital Ability rose 8.7 points to 73.6, with large gains among older Australians and people who did not complete school. Over the same period, and in the same national picture, school-age ICT literacy has gone backwards.

Reading the chart

Where Figure 2 compares years, this chart plots both year levels together, each point is one assessment round and the path runs from 2017 to 2025.

Between 2017 and 2022, Year 6 rose slightly while Year 10 fell sharply. From 2022 to 2025 the path heads down and to the left: Year 6 fell five points and Year 10 nine, nearly twice as far. By 2025 both are below their 2017 levels, with Year 10 down 17 points from 54% to 37%.

The shape tells us that the decline is not confined to one age group, and that it is steeper where students are older and more exposed to complex digital tasks. It does not tell us what caused it. The ACS summary of the report notes that students who use productivity applications often tend to score higher, while use of specialist applications is linked with lower scores, which suggests how students use technology may matter more than how much. That is an association from the report, not a result this page can test.

Beyond access

This returns us to whether Australian students have equal opportunities to access and benefit from digital technology. The evidence says opportunity is not equal, and that access, important as it is, is only the first of four conditions.

Conclusion sketch: the four conditions for equitable digital education, each tied to the finding that supports it and the evidence gap that remains.

Taken together, four findings stand out. First, proficiency is falling even as inclusion rises: Year 10 is at 37% and Year 6 at 50%, which shows that connecting students is not enough. Second, the region matters: inclusion declines with remoteness, and the very remote Access score is 21 points below the major cities. Third, students are concentrated, schools are scattered: nearly three in four students attend school in a major city, but the small remote schools that serve the rest face the thinnest advantage. Fourth, new tools are spreading unevenly, with the groups already less connected also less likely to use generative AI.

The practical reading is that equity in digital education depends on skills, support and teaching as much as on hardware. Priorities that follow from the evidence are reliable and affordable connections in remote and low-income communities, teaching of ICT and AI literacy that is deliberate across year levels. The most useful follow on would link device and connectivity measures to school-level outcomes, which would let future work test the questions within this report.
